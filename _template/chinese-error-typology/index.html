Improving Automatic Grammatical Error Annotation for Chinese Through Linguistically-Informed Error Typology

A Chinese-specific error typology for errant-style annotation of grammatical errors in L1 and L2 writing

COLING 2025

Yang Gu1,*, Zihao Huang1,*, Min Zeng2,†, Mengyang Qiu1,3,‡, Jungyeul Park1,4,‡

1Open Writing Evaluation, France 2TEKsystems, Canada 3Department of Psychology, Trent University, Canada 4Department of Linguistics, The University of British Columbia, Canada

*Equally contributed authors   †Work completed at UBC   ‡Corresponding authors

Paper (ACL Anthology) PDF

Abstract

Comprehensive error annotation is essential for developing effective Grammatical Error Correction (GEC) systems and delivering meaningful feedback to learners. This paper introduces improvements to automatic grammatical error annotation for Chinese. Our refined framework addresses language-specific challenges that cause common spelling errors in Chinese, including pronunciation similarity, visual shape similarity, specialized participles, and word ordering. In a case study, we demonstrated our system’s ability to provide detailed feedback on 12-16% of all errors by identifying them under our new error typology, specific enough to uncover subtle differences in error patterns between L1 and L2 writings. In addition to improving automated feedback for writers, this work also highlights the value of incorporating language-specific features in NLP systems.

Keywords Chinese grammatical error correction (CGEC) Grammatical error annotation Error typology ERRANT / ChERRANT Chinese spelling errors Pinyin and shape similarity De particle (的/地/得) L1 vs. L2 writing Learner corpora

Method

ChERRANT, the Chinese adaptation of the errant toolkit, labels operations as substitution (S), missing (M) or redundant (R) with a POS tag, and all spelling mistakes as the generic S:SPELL. On the L1 FCGEC validation data, its annotations matched manual annotations in 95% of 100 random erroneous sentences but only 73% of 30 sentences with two or more errors, mostly because long-span reordering is split into several substitutions. The paper re-implements Chinese errant with stanza POS tags, errant-style labels (R replacement, M missing, U unnecessary) and new Chinese-specific error types, assigned in this order:

  1. R:MULTI – pinyin similarity > α1 and shape similarity > α2 (e.g. 州/洲).
  2. R:DE – pinyin-similar replacement whose target is a de particle (的, 地, 得).
  3. R:PINYIN – other replacements with pinyin similarity > α1, computed from the edit distance between pinyin sequences.
  4. R:SHAPE – replacements with shape similarity > α2, computed by rendering both characters as font images and comparing them with a pretrained ResNet.
  5. R:CO / R:WO – source and target contain the same set of characters: a character-order error if the target is one word, otherwise a word-order error.
  6. M:DE – a missing de particle.

Both thresholds α1 and α2 are set to 0.9. All other edits keep their R, M or U label with a POS tag.

Main Results

  • Detailed feedback for 12–16% of errors. The new error types account for 12.12% of all annotated errors in L1 writing (FCGEC) and 16.78% in L2 writing (MuCGEC).
  • Spelling errors broken down. Spelling-related types make up 7.27% of all errors in L1 and 11.15% in L2.
  • L1 vs. L2 differences. Pinyin-similarity errors (0.0134 vs. 0.0321) and shape-similarity errors (0.0128 vs. 0.0361) are more than twice as frequent in L2, while multifaceted-similarity errors are close in both groups (0.0466 vs. 0.0433).
  • Limits of the previous tool. ChERRANT agreed with manual annotation in 95% of single-error sentences but 73% of multi-error sentences, with 23% of disagreements due to how ordering errors are represented.
