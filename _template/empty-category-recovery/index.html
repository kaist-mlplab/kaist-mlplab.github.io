Revisiting Absence with Symptoms that *T* Show up Decades Later to Recover Empty Categories

Removing and restoring null elements (empty categories) in the English, Chinese, and Korean Penn Treebanks with rules and a seq2seq model

arXiv 2024

Emily Chen1,*, Nicholas Huang1,*, Casey Robinson2,*, Kevin Xu1,*, Zihao Huang2, Jungyeul Park2

1Department of Computer Science, The University of British Columbia 2Department of Linguistics, The University of British Columbia

*Equally contributed authors

Paper (arXiv) PDF

Abstract

This paper explores null elements in English, Chinese, and Korean Penn treebanks. Null elements contain important syntactic and semantic information, yet they have typically been treated as entities to be removed during language processing tasks, particularly in constituency parsing. Thus, we work towards the removal and, in particular, the restoration of null elements in parse trees. We focus on expanding a rule-based approach utilizing linguistic context information to Chinese, as rule based approaches have historically only been applied to English. We also worked to conduct neural experiments with a language agnostic sequence-to-sequence model to recover null elements for English (PTB), Chinese (CTB) and Korean (KTB). To the best of the authors' knowledge, null elements in three different languages have been explored and compared for the first time. In expanding a rule based approach to Chinese, we achieved an overall F1 score of 80.00, which is comparable to past results in the CTB. In our neural experiments we achieved F1 scores up to 90.94, 85.38 and 88.79 for English, Chinese, and Korean respectively with functional labels.

Method

Penn-style treebanks mark unpronounced material with null elements: traces of A′ movement (*T*, the only type found in all three languages), NP traces (*), empty units (*U*) and null complementizers (0) in English, and *PRO*, *pro*, null operators (*OP*), right node raising (*RNR*), and predicate deletion (*?*, Korean) in the pro-drop languages. Parsers usually strip them. The paper studies both removal and restoration, using a post-processing approach that inserts null elements into trees parsed without them.

  1. Removal. Trees are produced with and without traces (Berkeley Neural Parser style for English and Chinese, and a similar procedure for Korean, for which no such parser existed); removal also drops function labels such as NP-SBJ.
  2. Rule-based restoration. For English, Campbell’s (2004) Government-Binding-based rules are re-implemented; for Chinese, new rules derived from the CTB bracketing guidelines insert *OP*/*T* in relative clauses, *PRO*, *pro*, and *RNR* from tree patterns such as ( CP ( IP VP ) DEC ).
  3. Language-agnostic seq2seq restoration. Trees are linearized and T5-small (about 60M parameters) translates a tree without null elements into a tree with them, without access to the words, for example
    (TOP (S (NP ... )NP (VP VBD (NP (NP NN )NP (SBAR (WHNP WDT )WHNP (S (VP MD (VP)VP )VP )S )SBAR )NP )VP SFN )S )TOP →
    (TOP (S (NP ... )NP (VP VBD (NP (NP NN )NP (SBAR (WHNP WDT )WHNP (S (NP *T* )NP (VP ... )VP )S )SBAR )NP )VP SFN )S )TOP (Figure 3 in the paper). Models are trained with and without function labels.
  4. Evaluation. Johnson’s (2002) PARSEVAL over null-element nodes only, re-implemented on top of the alignment-based jp-evalb so that terminals are aligned first.

Main Results

  • Rules for Chinese. The rule-based approach extended to the CTB reaches 80.00 average F1, improving over Chung and Gildea’s (2010) pattern matching for *PRO* (71.49 vs. 62.00) and *pro* (39.08 vs. 31.00).
  • Seq2seq across three languages. The T5 model reaches 88.67 / 85.38 / 88.79 average F1 for English / Chinese / Korean without function labels and 90.94 / 83.76 / 88.98 with them.
  • Neural beats rules on hard elements. Without lexical information, seq2seq still restores Chinese *PRO* and *pro* better than rules (82.03 and 63.79 vs. 71.49 and 39.08); Korean *pro* is much easier (91.61 / 92.11) because it need not be distinguished from *PRO*.
  • English rules remain strong. Campbell (2004) still leads in English (93.70 average; 91.90 for *T*), while seq2seq is better on *U* (99.61) and 0 (97.31).
