A Novel Alignment-based Approach for PARSEVAL Measures

Scoring constituency parses with PARSEVAL even when sentence boundaries and tokenization differ from the gold trees, by aligning sentences and words first

Computational Linguistics 2024

Eunkyul Leah Jo1,†, Angela Yoonseo Park2,†, Jungyeul Park2,∗

1The University of British Columbia, Department of Computer Science 2The University of British Columbia, Department of Linguistics

†Equal contribution   ∗Corresponding author

Paper (ACL Anthology) PDF Computational Linguistics (DOI) Code

Abstract

We propose a novel method for calculating PARSEVAL measures to evaluate constituent parsing results. Previous constituent parsing evaluation techniques were constrained by the requirement for consistent sentence boundaries and tokenization results, proving to be stringent and inconvenient. Our new approach handles constituent parsing results obtained from raw text, even when sentence boundaries and tokenization differ from the preprocessed gold sentence. Implementing this measure is our evaluation by alignment approach. The algorithm enables the alignment of tokens and sentences in the gold and system parse trees. Our proposed algorithm draws on the analogy of sentence and word alignment commonly used in machine translation (MT). To demonstrate the intricacy of calculations and clarify any integration of configurations, we explain the implementations in detailed pseudo-code and provide empirical proof for how sentence and word alignment can improve evaluation reliability.

Method

EVALB, the standard implementation of PARSEVAL, assumes gold and system files with the same number of sentences and identical terminal tokens, so parser output produced from raw text (with its own sentence splitting and tokenization) cannot be scored. The squib keeps the PARSEVAL definition of a correct constituent (same label and span) but adds an alignment preprocessing step:

  1. Sentence alignment (Algorithm 2). Gold (L) and system (R) sentences are compared with spaces removed; on a mismatch, sentences are accumulated until the next pair matches again, and the accumulated trees are aligned with each other.
  2. Word alignment (Algorithm 3). Within each aligned pair, mismatching tokens (e.g. ca n't vs. can not, or differing morphological segmentation) are accumulated until the following tokens agree; a per-language exception list covers frequent contractions and symbols.
  3. Constituent matching (Algorithms 1 and 4). Constituents (label, start, end, aligned tokens) are extracted from each aligned tree pair and true positives are counted as in PARSEVAL, giving precision, recall and F1.

Two assumptions make scoring well defined under morphological mismatches: (i) the whole-tree constituent counts as a true positive if the sentences are aligned and their root labels agree, and (ii) a subtree constituent counts as a true positive if its lexical items align and its phrase label is the same.

Main Results

  • Word mismatches. For gold This ca n't be right vs. system this can not be right, word alignment of {ca n't} and {can not} gives perfect precision and recall (5/5 each), even without the exception list; EVALB cannot evaluate this pair.
  • Morphological mismatches. For the Hebrew example of Tsarfaty et al. (2012), the pairs {H CL, CL} (‘the shadow’) and {H NEIM, HNEIM} (‘the pleasant’) are word-aligned, yielding precision 4/4 and recall 4/6.
  • Sentence mismatches. When the system splits one gold sentence into two, the merged trees obtain precision 5/8 and recall 5/7.
  • Same complexity as EVALB. Sentence and word alignment are linear, O(I + J) in the lengths of the gold and system sequences, and constituent matching is a tree traversal, O(N + E); the method therefore keeps EVALB's complexity and its PARSEVAL scores, enabling end-to-end evaluation of constituent parsers.
