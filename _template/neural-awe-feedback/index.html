Neural Automated Writing Evaluation with Corrective Feedback

An integrated system that scores L2 English essays on an overall score and eight rubrics (AWE) and returns grammatical error corrections (GEC)

arXiv 2024

Izia Xiaoxiao Wang1,*, Xihan Wu2,*, Edith Coates3,*, Min Zeng2, Jiexin Kuang2, Siliang Liu2, Mengyang Qiu4, Jungyeul Park2

1Linguistik Zentrum Zürich, Universität Zürich, Schweiz 2Department of Linguistics, The University of British Columbia, Canada 3Department of Mathematics, The University of British Columbia, Canada 4Department of Psychology, Trent University, Canada

*Equally contributed authors

Paper (arXiv) PDF Demo video

Abstract

The utilization of technology in second language learning and teaching has become ubiquitous. For the assessment of writing specifically, automated writing evaluation (AWE) and grammatical error correction (GEC) have become immensely popular and effective methods for enhancing writing proficiency and delivering instant and individualized feedback to learners. By leveraging the power of natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning algorithms, AWE and GEC systems have been developed separately to provide language learners with automated corrective feedback and more accurate and unbiased scoring that would otherwise be subject to examiners. In this paper, we propose an integrated system for automated writing evaluation with corrective feedback as a means of bridging the gap between AWE and GEC results for second language learners. This system enables language learners to simulate the essay writing tests: a student writes and submits an essay, and the system returns the assessment of the writing along with suggested grammatical error corrections. Given that automated scoring and grammatical correction are more efficient and cost-effective than human grading, this integrated system would also alleviate the burden of manually correcting innumerable essays. Keywords Automated writing evaluation (AWE) Automated essay scoring Grammatical error correction (GEC) Corrective feedback Second language (L2) writing Rubric / trait scoring ASAP / ASAP++ BEA-2019 Language education technology

System

  1. AWE (rubric scoring). Trained on ASAP and ASAP++ (eight prompts with overall and three to five rubric scores). The essays are first denoised with gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct (fixing UTF-8 encoding errors and replacing anonymized @PERSON-style entities), and ERRANT .m2 edits are used to undo any grammatical corrections the LLM introduced. RoBERTa-base is then trained as a regressor on scores normalized to 0–1, and evaluated by quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) in a cross-prompt setting.
  2. GEC (corrective feedback). fairseq seq2seq models with pre-trained BERT or BART, following Kaneko et al. (2020), trained on the BEA-2019 data (FCE, NUCLE, W&I+LOCNESS, Lang-8; 1,157,324 sentence pairs, augmented with spell-checked sources to 1,718,693 pairs).
  3. Interface. Results are merged into one view with inline edits and scores (Figure 2). A planned instructor workflow lets teachers adjust AWE and GEC output before it reaches students; extensions to Chinese and Korean GEC and multilingual rubrics are under way.

Main Results

  • GEC. The best GEC model (using BERT) reaches 65.29 F0.5 on the BEA-2019 test set.
  • AWE. Cross-prompt QWK averages 0.6143 on denoised (cleaned) essays versus 0.6047 on the original text, with the largest gain on Prompt 1 (0.6187 → 0.7344) and a drop on Prompt 2 (0.6308 → 0.5485).
  • Denoising. More than 5% of ASAP sentences have encoding problems or non-word entities, which motivated the LLM-based denoising step.
  • Integration. The system returns one overall score, eight rubric scores, and inline corrections for each submission; a questionnaire for learner and instructor perception studies is proposed (Table 2 in the paper).
