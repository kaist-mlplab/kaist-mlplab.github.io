Refined Evaluation for End-to-End Grammatical Error Correction Using an Alignment-Based Approach

jp-errant: sentence alignment lets errant-style GEC evaluation run on raw text with mismatched sentence boundaries, in English, Chinese, and Korean

COLING 2025

Junrui Wang1,*, Mengyang Qiu2,3,†, Yang Gu3, Zihao Huang3, Jungyeul Park1,3,†

1Department of Linguistics, The University of British Columbia, Canada 2Department of Psychology, Trent University, Canada 3Open Writing Evaluation, France

*Currently at TikTok, Canada   †Corresponding authors

Paper (ACL Anthology) PDF

Abstract

We propose a refined alignment-based method to assess end-to-end grammatical error correction (GEC) systems, aiming to reproduce and improve results from existing evaluation tools, such as errant, even when applied to raw text input—reflecting real-world language learners’ writing scenarios. Our approach addresses challenges arising from sentence boundary detection deviations in text preprocessing, a factor overlooked by current GEC evaluation metrics. We demonstrate its effectiveness by replicating results through a re-implementation of errant, utilizing stanza for error annotation and simulating end-to-end evaluation from raw text. Additionally, we propose a potential multilingual errant, presenting Chinese and Korean GEC results. Previously, Chinese and Korean errant were implemented independently for each language, with different annotation formats. Our approach generates consistent error annotations across languages, establishing a basis for standardized grammatical error annotation and evaluation in multilingual GEC contexts.

Keywords Grammatical error correction (GEC) GEC evaluation errant / M2 Sentence alignment Sentence boundary detection End-to-end evaluation Multilingual GEC Korean GEC Chinese GEC

Method

errant, the de facto standard GEC metric, compares gold and system edits sentence by sentence and therefore assumes both sides share the same sentence boundaries. When a GEC system works on raw learner text, its sentence splitter often disagrees with the gold segmentation. The paper borrows sentence alignment from machine translation to remove this assumption:

  1. Joint-preprocessing alignment. Gold and system sentences are compared with all spaces removed. Matching pairs are aligned directly; otherwise the shorter side is extended with its next sentence until the two match. Near-matches caused by different tokenization (e.g. can’t vs. ca n’t) are accepted with a Jaro-Winkler similarity extended with a suffix scale. The algorithm runs in linear time, O(m + n), instead of the cubic time of length-based MT aligners.
  2. Re-indexing. When sentences are concatenated, edit offsets are shifted by the token counts of the preceding sentences and redundant empty (noop) edits are removed, so the m2 files stay valid.
  3. jp-errant. errant is re-implemented on top of this alignment, replacing spaCy with stanza for POS tagging and parsing, keeping the English error classifier, and producing generic Missing/Unnecessary/Replacement edits for other languages.

As a reference, the authors also extend the Gale-Church aligner to m:n alignments (up to 11 sentences). On the Write & Improve development set the proposed aligner reaches a similarity of 1.0 at every CEFR level (A, B, C), versus 0.993–0.997 for the extended Gale-Church algorithm; 91.86% of aligned pairs are 1:1.

Main Results

  • Reproduces errant. With gold sentence boundaries on the W&I development set, jp-errant matches the original errant: overall F0.5 0.5582 vs. 0.5582 for GECToR and 0.5503 vs. 0.5501 for T5.
  • Evaluates raw-text output. With system-detected (stanza) sentence boundaries, which the original errant cannot score, overall F0.5 is 0.5444 for GECToR and 0.5269 for T5.
  • Korean. Without any Korean-specific error classifier, jp-errant gives F0.5 0.5334 (gold boundaries) and 0.5482 (system boundaries), versus 0.3019 with KAGAS, whose error detection and annotation have known limitations.
  • Chinese. jp-errant and ChERRANT agree closely (F0.5 0.2379 vs. 0.242 with gold boundaries); only about 4.56% of the errors fall into Chinese-specific types.
