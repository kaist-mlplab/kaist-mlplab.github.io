Chinese Grammatical Error Correction: A Survey

A review of Chinese GEC (CGEC) datasets, error annotation schemes, evaluation metrics, and systems, from rule-based methods to large language models

arXiv 2025

Mengyang Qiu, Qingyu Gao, Linxuan Yang, Yang Gu, Tran Minh Nguyen, Zihao Huang, Jungyeul Park*

*Corresponding author (jungyeul@kaist.ac.kr)  ·  open-writing-evaluation.github.io

Paper (arXiv) PDF

Abstract

Chinese Grammatical Error Correction (CGEC) is a critical task in natural language processing, addressing the growing demand for automated writing assistance in both second-language (L2) and native-speaker (L1) Chinese writing. While L2 learners face challenges in mastering complex grammatical structures, L1 users also benefit from CGEC in academic, professional, and formal contexts where writing precision is essential. This survey provides a comprehensive review of CGEC research, covering datasets and their annotation schemes, evaluation methodologies, and system developments. We examine widely used CGEC datasets, highlighting their characteristics, limitations, and the need for improved standardization. We also discuss dataset-specific annotation schemes and error representation formats, with particular attention to word segmentation ambiguity and the classification of Chinese-specific error types. Furthermore, we review evaluation metrics, focusing on their adaptation from English GEC to Chinese, including character-level scoring and the use of multiple references. In terms of system development, we trace the evolution from rule-based and statistical approaches to neural architectures, including Transformer-based models and large pre-trained language models. By consolidating existing research and identifying key challenges, this survey characterizes the current state of CGEC and outlines future directions, including the refinement of annotation standards for segmentation-sensitive error representation and the use of multilingual approaches to improve CGEC.

What the Survey Covers

  1. Datasets and error annotation (Section 2): CGED, NLPCC2018, MuCGEC, YACLC, FlaCGEC, FCGEC, CCTC, NLPCC2023 (NaCGEC), NaSGEC, and CCL CEFE, with their file formats, sentence examples, and annotation schemes.
  2. Evaluation in CGEC (Section 3): character-level versus word-level edit scoring, word order corrections, and single-reference mismatch.
  3. Systems (Section 4): machine translation approaches (SMT and NMT, including hybrid systems) and language model-based approaches (sequence tagging, encoder–decoder generation, and prompting of large language models).
  4. Appendices: automatic error annotation for Chinese (ChERRANT and a refined, linguistically informed typology published at COLING 2025), general GEC metrics (M2, I-measure, errant_compare, GLEU, reference-less metrics), and Chinese grammatical error detection.

Worked example: the evaluation unit changes the score

The source 我一钱没住过五星级旅馆，所以我很惊奇了。 (‘I had never stayed in a five-star hotel before, so I was very surprised.’) contains the erroneous 一钱 (should be 以前) and an unnecessary sentence-final 了. The reference fixes both; the system fixes only 一钱 → 以前 (Figure 12 in the paper).

  • Character level: gold edits are S(一→以), S(钱→前), R(了); the system makes the two substitutions, so P=2/2=1.0P = 2/2 = 1.0P=2/2=1.0, R=2/3=0.67R = 2/3 = 0.67R=2/3=0.67, F1=0.80F_1 = 0.80F1​=0.80, F0.5≈0.91F_{0.5} \approx 0.91F0.5​≈0.91.
  • Word level: gold edits are S(一钱→以前), R(了); the system makes one, so P=1.0P = 1.0P=1.0, R=1/2=0.5R = 1/2 = 0.5R=1/2=0.5, F1=0.67F_1 = 0.67F1​=0.67, F0.5≈0.83F_{0.5} \approx 0.83F0.5​≈0.83.

The same output thus gets different recall depending on whether edits are counted over characters or words. The survey concludes that CGEC evaluation is unit-sensitive edit-based evaluation, and that studies should state which unit is used for annotation, which for scoring, and how word order or span-level edits are handled.

Main Findings

  • Datasets are hard to compare. CGEC corpora differ in writer population (L2 learners vs. L1 writers), domain, segmentation basis, correction format, and how explicit their error labels are; the survey calls for more standardized annotation.
  • Multiple references vary widely. The average number of references per source ranges from 1.1 (NLPCC2018) and 2.3 (MuCGEC) to 10.6 (YACLC); multi-reference data such as MuCGEC gives fairer assessment and higher correlation with human judgments.
  • Character-level scoring is the default but not neutral. It avoids committing to one segmentation standard, but splits lexical corrections into several character edits and turns word order corrections into separate insertions and deletions.
  • Systems follow the English GEC trajectory, from rule-based and statistical detection, through SMT and NMT (sequence-to-sequence) correction, to pre-trained and large language models; reported scores depend heavily on dataset, metric, and preprocessing.
  • Future directions: consistent segmentation standards for segmentation-sensitive error representation, refined Chinese-specific error typologies, LLM fine-tuning or prompting, learner-adaptive feedback, and multilingual GEC.
